## Database Recommendation

For a healthcare patient management system (PMS), I would choose a relational database such as **MySQL** for the **system of record**. Core entities—patients, encounters, orders, diagnoses, medications, billing—require **strong ACID guarantees** so that multi‑step updates either complete fully or not at all. This matters for clinical safety (no partial orders), auditability (who changed what, when), and regulatory compliance. In terms of the CAP theorem, it is more acceptable for a PMS to tolerate short periods of reduced availability than to risk serving **inconsistent** medical data; thus, we should optimize for **Consistency** and **Partition tolerance** in the core OLTP path (a “CP‑leaning” stance).

**MongoDB** shines when the data model evolves rapidly or varies widely between records (e.g., storing semi‑structured intake forms or device telemetry). It can absolutely participate, but I would not make it the authoritative store for the clinical ledger unless there is a compelling, proven need. Instead, MongoDB (or a data lake) is great **adjacent** to the PMS for flexible schemas, fast iteration, and read‑optimized experiences (search, catalogs, notes with rich structure).

If the startup also needs a **fraud detection module**, I would adopt a **polyglot architecture**: keep **MySQL** as the ACID OLTP backbone, and **stream changes** (CDC/events) into analytics stores—MongoDB for flexible features, a warehouse for joins at scale, or a streaming system for real‑time scoring. Fraud analytics typically tolerates **eventual consistency** and benefits from schema flexibility, feature stores, and horizontal scale. In short: **MySQL for correctness and compliance; MongoDB and analytical systems for agility and detection.** This division of labor balances safety with speed and lets the team evolve each component independently.
